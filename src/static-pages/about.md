# About Build Log

I’m Mikhail Predtetchenski, usually known as Mike. Build Log is where I keep a public record of things I’m building, connecting, and troubleshooting.

Some of it is Windows and PowerShell. Some is AI assistants, memory, developer tools, and automation. Usually something comes up while I’m working, I try to understand it, and the process becomes a post.

I’m based in Central Texas. My background includes IT support, financial services, customer-facing technical work, and data center integration. I have a business degree and have spent years building, upgrading, and troubleshooting computers. I’m working toward IT and information security, and this log is part of turning that experience and independent learning into work I can demonstrate.

## What I’m working on

A lot of the work starts with an ordinary problem: Windows won’t boot after an update, OneDrive has far more files than I expected, or a publishing job says it succeeded when something failed.

AI adds another set of questions. I use several assistants, and I want the decisions from one conversation to carry into the next without explaining the whole project again. That has led me into shared memory (Hindsight), Obsidian, the Model Context Protocol (MCP), and connections between tools and services.

I’m also working through how to give assistants useful access while keeping control of what they can change. That includes permissions, coordinating reviewers and fixes, and checking whether a second opinion is independent when it has already seen the first draft.

Those projects are at different stages. Some are working setups, some are experiments, and some are still being written up. I try to say which is which.

## What goes into a post

I want to keep track of what I tried and why, including the approaches that went nowhere. Otherwise, a few weeks later I’m back to figuring out something I already spent a night on.

I usually start by looking at what already exists. Sometimes the answer is a setting or a tool I already have. Sometimes adapting something makes sense. Building another integration also means maintaining it, so I want a reason for doing that.

I use AI throughout these projects and the writing. It helps me investigate problems, write code, compare approaches, and turn working notes into drafts. Sometimes it does a substantial part of that work.

The posts start with things I’m actually working on: something broke, I tried an approach, or I wanted to find out whether a setup would work. I check the account against the available logs, files, and results, including where an assistant got something wrong. I decide what gets published, and I’m responsible for the claims.

That process can still miss things. I try to make clear what I tested, what I’m inferring, and what remains unfinished, so you have enough information to judge the result yourself.

## Why put it online?

Writing gives me a record I can return to and a reason to explain the work clearly. I want someone reading a post to understand the problem, follow the reasoning, and see the limits of the result.

If it helps someone solve a similar problem, question an assumption, or avoid a mistake I already made, that’s useful too.
